You just walked around the traffic cone. In the physical world, a traffic cone is a symbol of temporary, soft authority – it physically stops nobody, it just tells you where you’re allowed and not allowed to go. In the digital world, it’s not that different. You said you’re at least 18 years of age, nobody checked, and the answer never really mattered. No matter whether a law was declared that you must be an adult to read my essay, and no matter what age you really are, you could have stepped around that cone all the same. Everyone walked through, but only the readers who understand the system know that the gate meant nothing. For everyone else, the gate worked as authority. Freedom in an obligatory technological world rests on literacy and legibility, not abstention. You are free to the extent you understand and choose the systems that you depend on.

All this is not to say that traffic cones don’t work. When a road is blocked off by them, you rarely see a driver plow right through with their car, although by all means they could. This is because traffic cones hand the responsibility of that choice to humans. It is difficult to objectively define what it means to be human – each person you ask may answer completely differently. However, we can derive common meanings from the thoughts of the people, and from the design decisions of the systems put in place to keep them in order. To be a human is to have choice, to have individuality and judgment, all at the price of being accountable for the actions you take. Nations across the world have a vast range of systems for their inhabitants, with a spectrum of rights and freedoms granted to them, and a plethora of rules or regulations telling them what they can and cannot do. The system is tailored around this idea of humanity – it assumes you will act like an accountable person, and often, whether by constraint or design, hands the responsibility of choice to you instead of building it into the system.

If you ask somebody who turned away from a traffic cone why they didn’t step right around it, they may say that their conscience simply told them not to – they felt like it was wrong. The concept of a conscience, one’s moral fiber, requires consciousness, something we all feel but struggle to put into words. A widely accepted working definition for consciousness comes from Thomas Nagel’s 1974 paper “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” in The Philosophical Review. Nagel’s (1974) core claim is that an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is “something that it is like to be that organism.” In practice, this means that consciousness is a point of view. A camera produces a recorded output by sampling light, but there is nothing it’s like to be a camera. When you see a color, hear a song, or perceive just about anything, there is a felt quality to it from the inside. Nagel calls this the subjective character of experience. This defines consciousness as phenomenal experience, and even this definition is contested. However, this gives us an important perspective into the topics of today, with the rise of large language models – Nagel’s definition leans on whether there is something it is like to be the system, not how the system behaves. An LLM can appear to vividly describe what an experience feels like if you ask it, but this is the output of a neural network, and is not concrete evidence of anything greater on the inside. It is in this perceived humanity that the responsibility of this system again falls on the literacy of the user. When something talks like a human mind, people start treating it like one – we see many blindly trusting its judgment, becoming dependent on it, and shifting blame onto it when a mistake is inevitably made. The accountability the system hands to us with the technological resources we use holds inherent danger when this trust is shifted once again, and a system that seems to have a mind makes this handoff feel natural. It is up to the literacy of the user to realize nothing in it is accountable in the human sense, and the responsibility still lies with them.

Living in the world as it is today, opting out of technology is nearly impossible. University students are required to use Canvas to interface with their academic work, many workplaces strictly enforce multi-factor authentication apps like Duo, and those without phones will likely struggle socially. Being technologically free in this society does not mean opting out from this, even if you could; it means understanding and choosing the systems you depend on in your life. The beaten path is where the majority of infringements on a user’s freedom lie, taking by far the largest toll on the users of the default – and most widespread – consumer editions. Microsoft’s Windows operating system runs on the majority of the world’s desktops, coming pre-installed on most personal computers you can buy from a retailer. On Windows Home and Pro editions, the versions available to the average consumer, telemetry is non-negotiable, and configuring your system to not send diagnostic data to Microsoft is exclusively available on Enterprise, Education, and Server editions. The data collection controversy with Microsoft goes back a long way, with critics objecting to both the sheer amount of data collected and the inability to turn it off. Microsoft has gone even further to consolidate control over the average user’s data, requiring an internet connection on Windows setup and enforcing the connection of a Microsoft account, removing methods of creating local users and leaving that option only to technical users. This creates an environment where it is possible to leave the beaten path, but only with institutional access or technical skill.

There are places to turn for the privacy focused user, given they are willing and able – many privacy and security conscious individuals turn to free and fully open-source Linux distributions as their operating systems, host local services on their own hardware to truly own their data, employ VPNs to shift the trust of their internet traffic data away from their service providers, and meticulously vet every system they opt in to. In the age of information and with the internet at our fingertips, there are countless communities online that devote their time and effort to create and maintain systems or resources that aim to take back freedom and privacy, with most being open-sourced and free for anyone to use. This is not without its limits – achieving a semblance of technological freedom takes skill, time, and privilege, and this means it is not accessible to all. My personal setup runs Arch Linux, meaning every package on my machine was chosen by me and nothing was installed by default, and I run a home server setup that allows me to self host my services, even LLMs, so I can use AI on my own terms instead of sending my data to someone else’s server. I can do this because of my background in Computer Science, technical knowledge, time, and my ability to purchase the hardware and tools I need to maintain this. Yet, I still use Canvas and multi-factor authentication apps like everyone else. Furthermore, this web page is hosted on a server I don’t own or control. Even someone with the means to dive deep into this world cannot achieve full technological freedom, but the terms of some of the systems depended on can be chosen by the user.

Traffic cones, like the one you clicked through to read this, exist all around the internet. Recent events, however, leave it to be questioned whether that will remain the case. Age verification laws, like the UK’s Online Safety Act and a wave of US state laws, are replacing the traffic cone with brick walls – ID checks and even facial scans in some cases. The cone left you free but without protection; the wall protects you but requires you to surrender your identity. This is the tension that lies in the legislation and systems that are in place today, and this essay does not aim to resolve it. The most powerful asset to one’s freedom in a technological world is their literacy in the system, and not only understanding this tension but knowing what terms you’d rather accept can help shape the way you lead your life, make decisions, and own your data. You stepped around the traffic cone. Would you rather it had been a wall?
